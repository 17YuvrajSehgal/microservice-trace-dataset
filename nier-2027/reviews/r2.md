### Not submission-ready

The paper has an effective central observation: an LLM agent can have a mostly empty context window while its harness silently destroys evidence before the model sees it. The reported asymmetry—one list delivered in 4.7% of calls versus a sibling list delivered in 99.3%—is a compelling example of a potentially important software-engineering problem.

The paper also has a credible emerging research direction: evaluate not only what an agent produces and whether it reaches the correct answer, but what information survives each boundary between tools, model calls, agent phases, and persistent memory.

However, the paper currently moves between three claims that require different levels of evidence:

- Claim A — Mechanism: positional truncation can destroy late fields and invalidate serialized tool results.
- Claim B — Consequence: this information loss increases work and can affect diagnosis accuracy.
- Claim C — General significance: information attrition is a broader, undermeasured property of agent harnesses and should be included in software-engineering evaluations.

Your draft provides persuasive evidence for Claim A, some evidence for Claim B, and a plausible but not yet sufficiently validated argument for Claim C.

That distinction is likely to matter to an ICSE NIER reviewer.

## 4. Major concern #1: Your strongest accuracy claim is not causally established

Highest research priority

This is the most important scientific weakness in the paper.

Your abstract argues that serialization order partitions the diagnosis benchmark into solvable and unsolvable cases. It describes spawned culprits scoring 40–55 out of 60 and already-running culprits scoring 0–24 out of 60.

That sounds like a strong finding. But Section IV, especially the reversal experiment on pages 3–4, substantially complicates the interpretation.

Your own results report:

- The original defect destroys tool results and produces invalid JSON.
- Reversing the serialization order changes which fields survive the bound.
- The agent still localizes spawned culprits correctly in 15/15 runs.
- The agent recovers the culprit through sandboxed self-written analysis, with the culprit appearing in repeated aggregations.
- The culprit is unusually prominent in the trace, accounting for 14% of kernel events.
- On the harder resident-container cases, the repaired agent also fails to localize the culprit in the available probes.

You correctly acknowledge much of this in the paper. That honesty is a strength. But it leaves a central scientific question unresolved.

### What a skeptical reviewer will ask

If changing which fields survive does not change the answer, how much of the observed accuracy split is caused by serialization order, and how much is caused by the intrinsic difficulty of the fault class?

The paper itself admits that the original accuracy split remains correlational and that the causal accuracy experiment has not succeeded.

That is a serious limitation because the abstract and introduction currently give the impression that the positional delivery defect explains the benchmark's solvable and unsolvable halves.

### How to fix it

I recommend three changes.

A. Separate the demonstrated mechanism from the hypothesized accuracy consequence.

Your paper has strong evidence that positional truncation destroys information. It also has evidence that the agent may spend extra tool calls compensating for that loss. It does not yet have equally strong evidence that the delivery defect causes the reported resident-fault accuracy gap.

State that distinction explicitly in the abstract and introduction.

B. Make the next causal experiment the centerpiece of the future work.

Construct or select fault cases where the correct culprit is:

- Present only in the early field.
- Present only in the late field.
- Present in both fields.
- Recoverable through independent analysis.
- Not recoverable through independent analysis.

Then manipulate serialization order while keeping the underlying fault, tool content, model, and diagnosis task unchanged. Verify that the manipulation changes evidence availability, and measure whether it changes accuracy.

C. Include a manipulation check and a repaired baseline.

For each condition, report the actual number of relevant culprit records visible to the model, whether the payload parses, whether another tool can independently recover the culprit, and the final diagnosis. Without this information, reviewers cannot distinguish delivery failure from task difficulty or agent adaptation.

One important caution: you do not need to prove the entire accuracy hypothesis to publish a NIER paper. But you do need to make the paper's principal contribution consistent with the strongest evidence you actually have.

My recommendation is to make measured information loss and its behavioral cost the current contribution, and treat causal accuracy degradation as an important hypothesis requiring further validation.

## 5. Major concern #2: The numerical inconsistencies could undermine reviewer confidence

This is the next most urgent issue. A reviewer may forgive an emerging result that is small or incomplete. They are much less likely to overlook numbers that appear to describe different experiments without sufficient explanation.

I found several places where the paper needs reconciliation.

### Issue A — The compensation-cost percentages do not align cleanly

On page 3, the text reports +24% tool calls and +64% computations. Table II reports 53 tool calls and 21 computations for repair, versus 68 calls and 34 computations for the defect.

Calculating from the table:

- Tool calls: \\((68-53)/53 \approx 28.3\\%\\)
- Computations: \\((34-21)/21 \approx 61.9\\%\\)

The paper later gives 53 → 68 → 76 calls, which corresponds to approximately +28% and +43% relative to repair. The +43% figure is consistent with the final value, but the +24% figure needs an explanation or correction.

Fix: Recompute every percentage from the raw observations, specify the denominator and aggregation method, and use the same numbers in the abstract, body, and table.

### Issue B — The token-cost comparison is ambiguous

The abstract says the compensation tax reaches +14% tokens. Table II shows +5% in-tokens for the defect and +14% for reversed order. Later, the text describes the reversed condition as costing +14% tokens.

These numbers may refer to different conditions, but the paper needs to distinguish them consistently.

Fix: Define the baseline once, report absolute input tokens and relative changes for each condition, and state whether the measurements include all model calls or only selected calls.

### Issue C — Context utilization and total input tokens are not clearly distinguished

Page 2 reports a median context utilization of 6.6%, with the largest prompt in 120 runs reaching 23k tokens against a 400k window. Page 3 reports median input of 567k tokens and a maximum of 1.0M tokens against a 400k-per-call budget spread over approximately 60 calls.

These figures are not necessarily contradictory: one set could measure the largest individual prompt, while the other measures cumulative input tokens over an entire run. But that distinction is not sufficiently explicit.

Fix: Report these separately:

- Maximum context size of an individual model call.
- Median context utilization per call.
- Cumulative input tokens per run.
- Number of model calls per run.

Define exactly how the 6.6% statistic is calculated.

### Issue D — Multiple sample sizes need a clear experimental map

The paper reports 720 evaluation runs, 11,189 tool results, 3,827 truncations, 864 replayed results from 31 runs, 742 calls in the field-level delivery analysis, 234 of 554 workers exhausting their step budget, and a 120-cell ablation.

These may all be valid measurements from different datasets or experimental stages. However, readers need to know how they relate.

Fix: Add a compact experimental overview that maps each dataset to its purpose, unit of analysis, sample size, and overlap with other datasets.

The percentages are relatively easy to fix. The more important requirement is to make the evidence traceable: a reader should be able to move from each abstract statistic to a table, experimental condition, and clearly defined denominator.

### A further concern: The 15/15 results

In Table II, the repaired condition scores 14/15 while the defect and defect-with-reversed-order conditions score 15/15 on spawned culprits.

That is not evidence that the defect improves accuracy. With only 15 runs per condition, the difference is too small to support that interpretation, and repeated executions may not represent independent samples.

Present the result as evidence that the agent retained accuracy on these particular spawned-culprit cases while incurring additional work. Report uncertainty and explain how the runs were selected.

## 6. Major concern #3: The evaluation is small and the unit of analysis is unclear

The controlled ablation is a good idea. You register predictions, isolate multiple defects, and include negative controls. Those are all positive methodological choices.

However, the paper says the 120 cells use five distinct executions with repeats rather than large independent samples. Some reported conditions have only 10 or 15 runs.

A strict reviewer will ask:

1. What is a cell: a fault instance, a configuration, a run, or a configuration-by-fault combination?
2. Are repeated executions performed on the same underlying traces?
3. Do repeated runs use the same model version, temperature, tool state, and prompt?
4. Are the five distinct executions independent fault instances, or five executions of a smaller number of underlying cases?
5. How sensitive are the results to a different model seed or fault placement?
6. Are the reported medians and means computed over independent runs or repeated observations of the same cases?

### What I would change

You do not necessarily need hundreds of additional runs before submitting a NIER paper. The track explicitly permits promising preliminary results. But you need to make the current experiment interpretable.

Add a small experimental-design table:

| Element     | What to report                                                  |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Subject     | Agent, model/version, tools, and harness configuration          |
| Fault cases | Number of unique traces and fault instances per class           |
| Conditions  | Repaired, defect, reversed order, order-only control            |
| Replication | Unique instances versus repeated executions                     |
| Outcomes    | Correct localization, false alarms, tool calls, tokens          |
| Uncertainty | Confidence intervals or another appropriate uncertainty summary |

Where possible, use paired comparisons on identical fault instances across configurations. For binary outcomes, report the underlying counts and avoid overinterpreting small differences.

Most importantly, separate evidence of a deterministic mechanism from statistical evidence of a behavioral effect. The first can be compelling with a small number of demonstrations; the second requires more care about replication and uncertainty.

## 7. Major concern #4: The novelty argument is plausible but underdeveloped

The paper's conceptual distinction is good:

> An agent's context is the output of a pipeline its harness built.

That is memorable, and it gives the work a clear identity.

The risk is that a reviewer may interpret the contribution as a known engineering bug—truncating JSON strings—combined with a new name for the problem.

The paper needs to establish why information attrition is a distinct research problem, not merely a particular instance of context management, tool-output truncation, agent memory, or observability loss.

### What is missing?

The introduction mentions long-context research, harness architecture studies, and agent-failure taxonomies. But several related-work citations appear as unresolved ` [?]` placeholders, and the related-work discussion is not yet strong enough to establish a research gap.

I would expect a reviewer to ask:

- Have previous studies measured the difference between a tool's complete output and the information the model actually receives?
- How does your evidence yield differ from token utilization, tool success rate, or context compression ratio?
- What is new about instrumenting multiple boundaries rather than measuring truncation alone?
- Does your proposed five-stage pipeline apply to common agent frameworks, or only to your implementation?
- What does this model reveal that cannot be inferred from ordinary execution traces?

### How to strengthen novelty

Define three concepts rigorously.

1\. Evidence production

What counts as a unit of evidence? Is it a field, a record, a finding, a tool result, or a proposition about the system under diagnosis?

2\. Evidence delivery

What counts as evidence reaching the model? Is a field counted as delivered if it appears in a prompt but is malformed, incomplete, or impossible to interpret?

3\. Evidence survival to decision

How do you determine whether a piece of evidence actually reaches the final decision? Does it need to appear in a final agent message, be passed across a phase boundary, or be used in a decision-making computation?

These definitions matter because the paper's proposed evidence yield is central to its contribution.

For example, a tool result might be delivered as valid JSON but never inspected by the model. Conversely, a result could be truncated yet the model might reconstruct the missing information through another tool. Your metric should distinguish these cases.

I would also avoid describing the five-stage pipeline as something that all harnesses implement identically. The broad idea that evidence passes through transformations is general; the precise stages and observables may vary by architecture.

A stronger novelty claim would be:

> We propose an evidence-attrition model for agent harnesses that distinguishes loss at delivery boundaries from failures of reasoning over delivered evidence, and show how these losses can be measured independently of context-window utilization.

Only use that wording if your instrumentation actually supports those distinctions.

## 8. Major concern #5: Generalization beyond one agent is asserted more strongly than demonstrated

The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section VI:

> One harness, one domain.

That is an appropriate admission. The problem is that the abstract and introduction sometimes sound more general than the empirical scope warrants.

Your empirical setting is kernel-trace root-cause analysis, with fault injection and a specific agent harness. That is useful evidence, but it does not establish how often this occurs across software-engineering agents generally.

### My recommendation

Do not attempt a broad multi-framework evaluation at the expense of fixing the main experiment. Instead, add one modest, high-value generalization check if feasible:

- Test a second tool-output shape, such as a list of services, log records, or structured diagnostic findings.
- Test a second bounding strategy, such as suffix slicing or a token-aware structural limit.
- Or replay representative outputs from another agent harness.

Even a small cross-case demonstration could help establish that the problem is about the interaction between positional bounds and structured evidence, rather than an idiosyncrasy of one tool.

If you cannot add this experiment, explicitly label the broader pipeline model as a proposed general framework and the empirical result as a case study. NIER can accommodate that, provided the claims are calibrated.

## 9. Major concern #6: The paper's proposed metric needs a more precise definition

The proposed evidence yield is potentially one of the most valuable contributions. At present, however, it is introduced as the ratio of evidence reaching the decision to evidence produced.

That sounds straightforward, but a reviewer will immediately ask how the numerator and denominator are counted.

Suppose a tool produces 100 process records. The harness drops 40. The agent retrieves those records through another tool, but only 20 are relevant to the diagnosis. How many pieces of evidence were produced, delivered, and ultimately used?

There is no single obvious answer unless the paper defines its unit of measurement.

### A better measurement model

I would define separate stage-level measures rather than relying on a single ratio.

| Metric                | Suggested definition                                                                       | What it reveals                                    |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Delivery completeness | Evidence units delivered ÷ evidence units produced                                         | How much evidence is lost before delivery          |
| Structural validity   | Delivered results that remain parseable ÷ results produced                                 | Whether transformations break the data format      |
| Retention rate        | Evidence units retained across turns ÷ evidence units delivered                            | Loss caused by conversation or memory trimming     |
| Handoff completeness  | Findings transferred to the next phase ÷ findings produced                                 | Whether agent phases lose work                     |
| Decision availability | Relevant evidence units available to the decision-maker ÷ relevant evidence units produced | Whether diagnostically useful information survives |

These are suggested refinements, not metrics already validated by your study.

The last metric requires ground truth or a defensible relevance annotation. Otherwise, it measures total evidence availability rather than diagnostic evidence availability.

### Do not overclaim what the metric predicts

Your registered hypothesis H4 says evidence yield predicts accuracy better than context utilization. The reported results establish that context utilization can remain low despite delivery loss, but I do not see a sufficiently clear predictive comparison demonstrating that evidence yield outperforms context utilization across independent cases.

You have two choices:

- Preferred: Report the predictive comparison if the existing data support it. Specify the observations, outcomes, baselines, and evaluation procedure.
- Otherwise: Present H4 as a hypothesis for future validation, rather than a demonstrated finding.

This matters because a useful measurement framework and a validated predictor of correctness are two different contributions.

## 10. Major concern #7: The practical fix is under-evaluated

The paper demonstrates that row-aware bounding preserves structured fields better than a flat prefix slice. That is intuitive and technically credible.

But the reader still does not know how much this repair improves the agent as a whole.

You need to separate two outcomes:

1. Mechanical repair: Does the output remain parseable, and are the required fields preserved?
2. Agent-level benefit: Does the repair improve diagnosis, reduce repeated tool calls, lower token consumption, or reduce false conclusions?

The paper reports that some defects are repaired and that extra computations appear under the defect condition. But the complete repaired-versus-defective comparison is not sufficiently systematic.

### The minimum useful comparison

Compare the deployed behavior, the repaired behavior, and the reversed-order diagnostic control on identical underlying cases.

Report:

- Percentage of valid tool outputs.
- Percentage of relevant evidence delivered.
- Diagnosis accuracy.
- Tool calls and cumulative input tokens.
- Wall-clock time.
- Repair overhead, including the effect of structural processing.

The row-aware bound should not be treated as automatically superior in every respect. For example, dropping complete rows may still remove the one record needed for diagnosis. It may preserve valid structure while losing relevant content.

That is why the paper should compare both structural validity and evidence completeness, not merely valid JSON versus invalid JSON.

## 11. Major concern #8: The “84% of wall-clock time” claim needs stronger context

Section V argues that attrition can increase cost and reduce correctness without noticeably changing wall-clock time because the agent spends approximately 84% of its time waiting for model requests.

This is an interesting observation, and it strengthens the case for measuring more than latency.

But a reviewer may ask whether this is a stable property of the evaluated agent or a measurement specific to your environment.

Clarify:

- How many runs contribute to the 84% estimate?
- Is the statistic a mean or median?
- Does it include model queueing, network latency, retries, and tool execution?
- Are the defective and repaired configurations compared under the same inference conditions?
- Does the compensation tax increase total model calls, and therefore potentially increase wall-clock time, even if the difference is small?

I would keep this section, but frame it as an observation from the evaluated system rather than a general law about agentic systems.

### Writing: what works and what I would change

## 14. How I would rewrite the paper's central argument

I would organize the four-page main text around one coherent argument rather than giving equal weight to every result.

### Proposed argument structure

1. Problem: Context-window capacity does not tell us how much tool-produced evidence survives the harness.
2. Observation: In the evaluated kernel-trace agent, a positional bound disproportionately removes late structured fields, even when the context window is mostly unused.
3. Measurement: Introduce a pipeline model and stage-level attrition measurements, distinguishing malformed delivery from evidence lost at later stages.
4. Evidence: Show the deterministic delivery mechanism, replay recorded results, and report a controlled ablation demonstrating the effect on tool calls and computation.
5. Implication: Agent evaluations should report evidence delivery and retention alongside accuracy, token use, and latency.
6. Open question: Whether this information loss systematically causes diagnosis errors across fault classes and other harnesses remains to be tested.

This structure would preserve the paper's novelty while making the level of evidence clear at every step.

It also makes the contribution easier to evaluate under NIER's criteria: the new idea is the pipeline-level measurement perspective, the preliminary result is the demonstrated delivery failure and its observed cost, and the future work tests its broader implications.

## 15. A stronger abstract direction

I would revise the abstract so that the demonstrated mechanism is the central result, while the unresolved causal question is reported honestly. Here is a possible version based on the results in your draft; verify every statistic against the underlying logs before using it.

**Context Is a Pipeline, Not a Window: Information Attrition in LLM Agent Harnesses**

Long-context LLM agents are commonly evaluated in terms of context capacity and final-task accuracy. These measures can miss information lost before a model receives tool results. We investigate this problem in a tool-using agent for kernel-trace root-cause analysis. Across 720 evaluation runs, 3,827 of 11,189 tool results were truncated, and every truncated result was invalid JSON. The context window had a median utilization of 6.6%, showing that substantial delivery loss can occur even when model context capacity is not the binding constraint. In recorded results, positional truncation disproportionately removed late fields: one list reached the model in 4.7% of calls, compared with 99.3% for a sibling list. Replaying 864 recorded tool results confirmed that results exceeding the configured bound became unparseable under prefix slicing, whereas row-aware truncation preserved structural validity. A controlled ablation further showed that the delivery defect increased tool use and agent-written computation, while the tested agent retained localization accuracy on spawned-culprit cases by recovering the relevant information through alternative analyses. The causal effect of delivery loss on accuracy remains unresolved for harder resident-culprit cases. These observations motivate measuring information attrition at agent-harness boundaries alongside accuracy, token consumption, and latency. Future work will validate the proposed measurements across additional harnesses and fault classes.

Important: This is a proposed revision, not a verified replacement. It deliberately removes the claim that the defect has already been shown to cause the benchmark's accuracy split. It also omits the compensation percentages until they are reconciled.

I would keep the novel title, the striking context-utilization observation, the field-delivery asymmetry, and the controlled-ablation result. Those are the strongest elements of the current paper.
